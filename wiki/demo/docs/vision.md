# Fieldnote: a reader returns to a note without paying for it

## Audience
One person who works across several tasks a day and keeps notes to come
back to: a developer, a writer, a student between lectures.

## Non-users
Teams that need shared notebooks with roles and permissions; the product
is one person's home first.

## Problem
A note is written in one context and read in another. The reader arrives
without the day's thread, and a page that asks for effort — small type,
broken links, layout that shifts — loses them (S-001).

## Alternatives and difference
People keep notes in editors and big shared notebooks today; those are
built for writing first. Fieldnote treats the return — the reading half —
as the product.

## Key journeys
- Nadia, who writes software: she drops a half-formed thought into a note
  at noon, opens the same note three days later while talking to a
  colleague, and reads it without fixing anything first.
- Tomás, who studies: he keeps reading lists and questions; each evening
  he opens yesterday's note and continues where it stopped.

## Outcome
Opening an old note shows it readable in place: the text sits on the
shared tokens, the links resolve, and nothing moves while it settles.

## Success signal
A reader who returns to a two-week-old note reads it without editing or
reformatting it first; the counter-signal to watch is time spent writing,
which must not grow to buy the reading.

## Exclusions
Multi-user editing, publishing to the web, and an editor of its own: the
writing stays in any editor the writer already uses.

## Constraints and assumptions
Notes are Markdown files in a git repository; the reading happens in the
local wiki. The assumption that the return is the expensive half is a
hypothesis the product keeps on record (H-001).
